# The company and its product

Soil Acoustics Ltd sells a hand-held meter and a subscription that scores the sounds recorded by its soil probe. The company grew out of a collaboration between soil scientists at the University of Warwick and ecologists at Baker Consultants. [SRC-002](sources.md#src-002), [SRC-007](sources.md#src-007), [SRC-049](sources.md#src-049)

## Origins and field use

The original problem was the time it takes to count earthworms. In July 2023, Warwick said that sorting a few spadefuls of soil by hand could take over an hour. Pilot work had shown that a probe could pick up underground sounds. Warwick and Baker wanted to leave the animals in the soil and compare their sounds with a reference library. The feasibility study was already under way when Baker Consultants’ directors set up the commercial company in 2024. ## What a buyer gets

The Soil Acoustic Meter (SAM) uses a contact microphone and a probe. The user records at several GPS-tagged points, uploads the files by computer, and receives a proprietary Soil Acoustic Quality Index (SAQI) for each recording and a mean for the batch. The portal can export comparisons as PDFs. The public guide shows the controls, location map and upload screens on pages 4–10. A worked scorecard with field observations would help readers understand the numbers. [SRC-003](sources.md#src-003)

The regular prices above exclude VAT and delivery. The supplier lists the meter at £830 plus VAT. The batch equivalents are calculated using the guide’s minimum of ten samples per setting. A ten-point visit entails 30 minutes of recording alone, before moving, setup and upload. A field with several distinct habitats may need multiple batches. The guide advises avoiding rain and nights below 10°C, and recommends wind below 10 mph. This can limit the number of comparable sampling days. [SRC-015](sources.md#src-015), [SRC-016](sources.md#src-016), [SRC-017](sources.md#src-017), [SRC-003](sources.md#src-003)

**Groundswell show offer.** On 22 November 2026 the shop also listed one meter and the first year of Standard access at **£830**, £290 below the regular package. The promotional page gave no end date. Eligibility and the final checkout price were unverified. [SRC-043](sources.md#src-043)

**Rights to data affect the choice of plan.** The published terms state that the company owns uploaded recordings, metadata and derived datasets. They grant users a limited licence for non-commercial and internal research or educational use of their copies and require written consent for use of SAQI or derived scores. Access to the portal depends on a valid subscription. A consultant promising a client a reusable dataset, or a researcher planning an open publication, should obtain written confirmation covering that use before collecting data. [SRC-018](sources.md#src-018)

## What has been tested

The supplier describes SAQI as a measure of a recording’s acoustic complexity. In September 2026 it reported a database of more than 4,000 recordings. If a field scores higher on a return visit, has the number or variety of animals changed? Answering that question requires sampling the animals on the same ground alongside the recordings. [SRC-002](sources.md#src-002), [SRC-004](sources.md#src-004), [SRC-019](sources.md#src-019)

At Denton Reserve in West Yorkshire, Baker Consultants surveyed ten sites on two visits in July 2025. The team reported a statistically significant difference in acoustic complexity among sites even at one visit. The sites already spanned different habitats, including improved pasture and native woodland, when monitoring began. The result gives the estate a baseline for future visits. The authors give no paired animal counts or per-site scores and do not identify which index or recorder produced the Denton result. [SRC-054](sources.md#src-054)

In the same article, the developers describe a separate 2025 pilot with about 6,000 records across 11 countries. They report a statistically significant difference between the two farming systems they describe as regenerative and traditional. They give neither the direction nor the size of that difference, and do not say how many farms contributed the recordings. A grower cannot use the result to predict how much a named change in practice would affect a field. [SRC-054](sources.md#src-054)

A Royal Holloway-led doctoral project proposes checking commercial-meter recordings against soil animals in Finland. Researchers would record in forest plots planted with one or several tree species, sample the animals there and compare several acoustic indices with what they find. Soil Acoustics is the industry partner. The project description gives the study design and leaves open how well any index will track the animals. [SRC-051](sources.md#src-051)

Baker Consultants has also investigated whether it can recognise the sound wireworms make by rubbing body parts together. In a separate UK patent application published in June 2026, it reports **89% accuracy** for a model using three-second clips, with 70% of the labelled recordings used for training and 30% for testing. The application gives no result for previously unseen farms and no accuracy estimate for the commercial SAQI score. [SRC-050](sources.md#src-050)
