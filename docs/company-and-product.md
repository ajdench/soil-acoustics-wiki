# The company and its product

Soil Acoustics Ltd sells a handheld recorder and a subscription to analyse its recordings. A farmer can use the Soil Acoustic Meter (SAM) to revisit the same patch of ground and compare how it sounds over time. The company grew out of work by Baker Consultants and the University of Warwick on a more practical way to survey soil animals. [SRC-002](sources.md#src-002), [SRC-007](sources.md#src-007), [SRC-049](sources.md#src-049)

## Origins and field use

In July 2023, Warwick reported that sorting earthworms from a few spadefuls of soil by hand could take more than an hour. A probe had already picked up underground sounds in pilot tests. The researchers wanted to compare those sounds with a reference library while leaving the animals in place. Baker Consultants led the April 2023 to March 2025 study. UK Research and Innovation records grant offers of £116,565 to Baker and £141,193 to Warwick, £257,758 in total. [SRC-049](sources.md#src-049), [SRC-008](sources.md#src-008)

During the study, Baker Consultants’ directors established Soil Acoustics Ltd to bring the meter to market. Companies House lists it as an active private company in Matlock, Derbyshire, incorporated on 16 February 2024 (company number 15493769). Its registered directors are Andrew Baker, Saffron Baker and Gavin Ward. [SRC-005](sources.md#src-005), [SRC-006](sources.md#src-006), [SRC-007](sources.md#src-007)

The project team reports building a prototype, collecting more than 2,000 recordings and developing an algorithm to analyse them. It also reports controlled experiments and names Rothamsted, Wildfarmed, the National Trust, JoJo’s Vineyard and Yeo Valley among its trial sites. The published UK patent names Baker Consultants Ltd as the patent holder and Andrew Baker and Carlos Abrahams as inventors. [SRC-009](sources.md#src-009), [SRC-011](sources.md#src-011)

**Newhouse Farm shows how another team used the meter.** Sustainable Acoustics began a pilot at the Hampshire farm in September 2025. At its June 2026 open day, the team compared birds and bats recorded above an agroforestry plot and a control area. The Institute of Acoustics scheduled Kristina Baker, Andy Officer and Peter Rogers to speak about the pilot on 14 September 2026. These accounts document use of the soil probe. Neither gives soil scores paired with earthworm counts. [SRC-010](sources.md#src-010), [SRC-053](sources.md#src-053)

Veldshop lists the meter for sale, and Natural Apptitude describes building its analysis portal. Soil Acoustics names Ruinart and Wildfarmed as clients. Its website groups customers and partners together, and its micro-entity accounts give no sales or subscriber totals. The number of paying customers and renewals therefore remains unknown. [SRC-012](sources.md#src-012), [SRC-013](sources.md#src-013), [SRC-002](sources.md#src-002), [SRC-014](sources.md#src-014)

## What a buyer gets

The probe’s contact microphone picks up vibrations in the soil. The operator records at several points, then connects the meter to a computer and uploads the recordings and their GPS locations. The portal returns a **Soil Acoustic Quality Index (SAQI)** for each recording and an average for the batch. An operator can listen to individual recordings, compare batches and download a comparison as a PDF. Pages 4–10 of the guide show the controls, locations and upload screens. [SRC-003](sources.md#src-003)

The guide recommends at least ten samples per setting, following a W-shaped route. At three minutes per recording, that is 30 minutes of recording time before setup, walking between points or uploading. A field with distinct habitats may need several batches. The guide advises against sampling in rain or after overnight temperatures below 10°C. It recommends wind speeds below 10 mph. [SRC-003](sources.md#src-003)

**Who can use the results?** The published terms say Soil Acoustics owns uploaded recordings, their metadata and derived datasets. Users receive a limited licence for copies of their own data and metadata for non-commercial and internal research or educational purposes. Use of SAQI or derived scores requires written consent. Portal access requires a current subscription. A consultant preparing client reports or a researcher planning to publish scores can use these terms to identify the permissions to confirm in writing. [SRC-018](sources.md#src-018)

## What has been tested

SAQI summarises the acoustic complexity of a recording. Soil Acoustics says higher scores indicate greater abundance and variety of invertebrates. In September 2026 it reported a database of more than 4,000 recordings. Testing the biological claim requires a second observation at the recording point, such as a count of the animals found there. [SRC-002](sources.md#src-002), [SRC-004](sources.md#src-004), [SRC-019](sources.md#src-019)

**Denton Reserve.** Baker Consultants surveyed ten sites at the West Yorkshire reserve on two visits in July 2025. The team reported that acoustic complexity differed significantly among sites even on one visit. The sites already differed in habitat, from improved pasture to native woodland. The survey gives the estate a starting point for future comparisons. The authors did not publish scores for individual sites or paired animal counts, or identify which recorder or index produced this result. [SRC-054](sources.md#src-054)

**International farming pilot.** In the same article, the developers describe about 6,000 records from 11 countries in 2025. They report a statistically significant difference between the farming systems they describe as regenerative and traditional. They give no direction or size for the difference and no count of participating farms. A grower therefore cannot use the result to predict what a change in practice would do on one field. [SRC-054](sources.md#src-054)

**A planned test in Finland.** A Royal Holloway-led doctoral project proposes recording forest plots planted with one or several tree species, sampling the soil animals and comparing the resulting counts with acoustic indices. It names SAM as the recorder and Soil Acoustics as the industry partner. The published description sets out the study design. [SRC-051](sources.md#src-051)

**A separate wireworm test.** A June 2026 patent application describes Baker Consultants’ work on the sound wireworms make by rubbing body parts together. The team reports 89% accuracy for a model that classified three-second clips. It used 70% of its labelled recordings for training and 30% for testing. This figure applies to that classification task. The application gives no accuracy estimate for SAQI or for wireworm detection on previously unseen farms. [SRC-050](sources.md#src-050)

For a different sampling approach, Biofonic is trialling a sensor that can stay in the field for weeks, collecting sound, temperature and moisture at one point. Its developer offers a trial waitlist. SAM’s portable design lets an operator sample several locations in one session. The [monitoring systems comparison](monitoring-systems.md) covers the equipment and services available for each approach.
