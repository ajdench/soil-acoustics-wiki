# What soil sounds tell us

A soil recording can reveal movement near a probe. Three different questions follow. Did activity change? Were more animals or more kinds of animal present? Did the soil’s condition improve? Research supports the first conclusion in controlled settings and sometimes the second in the field. A score for overall soil health needs its own comparison with biological, physical and chemical measurements. [SRC-019](sources.md#src-019), [SRC-023](sources.md#src-023), [SRC-024](sources.md#src-024), [SRC-044](sources.md#src-044), [SRC-045](sources.md#src-045)

## What makes a soil recording change?

**One active animal can generate many events.** Lacoste, Ruiz and Or compared a soil-filled glass cell containing one burrowing earthworm with controls. Over seven days the worm cell produced roughly ten times as many acoustic events. Time-lapse images showed where it burrowed. Separate experiments associated emissions with growing maize roots. Their sensors were designed for 40–450 kHz. This is evidence that different biological processes generate vibrations. It gives no measurement of how much root activity enters a commercial meter recording. [SRC-044](sources.md#src-044)

**One species can raise a diversity-related index.** In a Swedish experiment, researchers recorded groups of 0, 1, 5, 10, 25 or 50 red wood ants for three minutes, five times per group size. All four sound measures rose as the number of ants increased, although only one species was present. Group size explained 45% of the variation in the Bioacoustic Index. In separate outdoor recordings, active mounds also differed from abandoned ones. A higher index can therefore be a useful measure of activity even when species richness stays the same. [SRC-046](sources.md#src-046)

Soil conditions affect detection too. A controlled geophone experiment detected earthworm movements more frequently in wetter soil. On a farm, a change after rain could reflect changes in animal behaviour, vibration transmission or both. Wind, footsteps and machinery can also enter a recording. Moisture, temperature, recent rain and nearby field operations belong beside the score. [SRC-004](sources.md#src-004), [SRC-022](sources.md#src-022), [SRC-023](sources.md#src-023)

## When does the signal track the animals present?

| Study | Finding | What limits the inference? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Mount Bold, South Australia, 2024.** Two cleared, two revegetated and two remnant plots, 240 recordings over five days and hand-sorted invertebrate counts. | Both the Acoustic Complexity Index and the Bioacoustic Index increased with animal abundance. Only the complexity index showed a statistically significant association with the number of different invertebrate types. | The hand-sort used mixed taxonomic ranks. Researchers recorded broad groups and also distinguished unnamed types within groups, including several ants and spiders. [SRC-019](sources.md#src-019), [SRC-034](sources.md#src-034), [SRC-055](sources.md#src-055) |
| **Greno Woods, South Yorkshire, 2023.** Three restored and three deforested plots, with recordings in the ground and in a sound-attenuating chamber. | Both indices tracked the abundance of animals sorted from soil. Neither had a statistically significant association with the number of different groups found. | The two indices distinguished restored from deforested plots in the chamber. Neither showed a detectable restoration effect in the ground. Coauthor Carlos Abrahams was affiliated with Baker Consultants. [SRC-045](sources.md#src-045) |
| **Oxfordshire pasture, 2026 research briefing.** Twenty-four hours of recording at each of ten grassland sites, paired with invertebrate sampling. | Several indices correlated with earthworm counts. | Management history, soil and moisture varied. A full day samples activity a three-minute visit can miss. [SRC-021](sources.md#src-021) |
| **Constructed soil soundscapes, 2024.** Fourteen indices were tested on one-minute recordings with known numbers and types of sound events. | Combinations of indices predicted event counts more successfully than the variety of sounds across recordings or how evenly they occurred. | Sound events count occurrences. Several can come from the same individual. A coauthor was affiliated with Baker Consultants. [SRC-020](sources.md#src-020) |

The Mount Bold paper reports repeated-measures correlations with abundance of 0.67 (0.18 to 0.90) for the complexity index and 0.60 (0.22 to 0.83) for the Bioacoustic Index. For taxon richness the corresponding figures were 0.69 (0.16 to 0.93) and 0.45 (−0.12 to 0.84). Parentheses give the ranges reported alongside the correlations. The last range includes zero (*p* = 0.09), so that result leaves the direction of the association uncertain. [SRC-034](sources.md#src-034)

A 2026 experiment asked whether animals can be distinguished by *how* they move. Annells and colleagues recorded ten individuals from each of six species, one at a time for five minutes on an aluminium plate. Their 19 sound features differed overall by species, but garden worms and snails could not be separated statistically in the pairwise comparison (*p* = 0.128). The study used neither mixed communities nor soil. It reports no field identification accuracy that a SAM user could apply to an unknown recording. [SRC-047](sources.md#src-047)

## Validation of the commercial score

The Soil Acoustic Meter (SAM) measures vibrations. Its portal converts recordings into the proprietary Soil Acoustic Quality Index (SAQI). The developer reports controlled experiments and more than 2,000 recordings. The studies above used other sensors, other indices or other recording periods. The reviewed public material provides no independent estimate of how accurately SAQI predicts fauna counts or richness on farms outside its development dataset. It also leaves open how large a score change exceeds repeat-measurement variation. [SRC-002](sources.md#src-002), [SRC-003](sources.md#src-003), [SRC-009](sources.md#src-009), [SRC-020](sources.md#src-020), [SRC-023](sources.md#src-023), [SRC-045](sources.md#src-045)

## How to investigate a change in score

1. **Check repeatability.** Repeat the supplier’s sampling pattern at marked points under comparable conditions. Keep individual readings as well as the batch mean. Repeat placement or change operators at a subset to see how much ordinary sampling changes the result. Until that spread is known, a difference between two means has no reliable action threshold. [SRC-003](sources.md#src-003), [SRC-004](sources.md#src-004), [SRC-022](sources.md#src-022)
2. **Check what the score predicts.** If the claim is about abundance, pair the recordings with standardised animal counts at the same points. If it is about richness, also identify the groups present and report the level of identification. Test the score on entire farms held out of its development data. Report the error and uncertainty for each outcome, including quiet points. [SRC-019](sources.md#src-019), [SRC-020](sources.md#src-020), [SRC-045](sources.md#src-045), [SRC-046](sources.md#src-046)
3. **Check the management outcome separately.** Follow a treated area and a comparable untreated area before and after a change. If the goal is better structure, measure structure. If the goal is nutrient availability, measure the relevant nutrient. Use a change in the recordings to identify places worth investigating. [SRC-021](sources.md#src-021), [SRC-023](sources.md#src-023), [SRC-024](sources.md#src-024)
