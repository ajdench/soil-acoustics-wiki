# What soil sounds tell us

A single earthworm can make many sounds. Researchers have found that busier recordings sometimes come from soil containing more animals. How many *kinds* of animal live there is harder to infer. The evidence for either conclusion depends on what researchers counted beside the probe. Overall soil health also includes physical and chemical properties that a recording alone cannot establish. [SRC-019](sources.md#src-019), [SRC-023](sources.md#src-023), [SRC-024](sources.md#src-024), [SRC-044](sources.md#src-044), [SRC-045](sources.md#src-045)

## What makes a soil recording change?

**Movement and growth.** In a seven-day experiment, a soil-filled glass cell containing one burrowing earthworm produced about ten times as many acoustic events as the controls. Time-lapse images showed where the worm burrowed. Separate experiments detected vibrations from growing maize roots. The researchers used ultrasonic sensors designed for 40–450 kHz. How much root activity a farm meter picks up is a separate question. [SRC-044](sources.md#src-044)

**The number of active animals.** Swedish researchers made five three-minute recordings at each of six group sizes, from no ants to 50 red wood ants. All four sound measures rose with group size. The number of ants explained 45% of the variation in one measure, the Bioacoustic Index. Active and abandoned mounds also sounded different outdoors. The laboratory result shows how a score can rise while the same single species is making the sound. [SRC-046](sources.md#src-046)

**Soil conditions and background noise.** A vibration sensor detected earthworm movement more often in wetter experimental soil. A change after rain could reflect animal behaviour, how vibrations travel through the soil, or both. Wind, footsteps and machinery can also enter a recording. Notes on moisture, temperature, recent rain and nearby work help explain differences between visits. [SRC-004](sources.md#src-004), [SRC-022](sources.md#src-022), [SRC-023](sources.md#src-023)

## When does the signal track the animals present?

Two woodland studies compared recordings with invertebrates counted by hand. **Abundance** means how many animals the team counted. **Richness** means how many different types it distinguished. Both teams used the Acoustic Complexity Index (ACI), which measures changes in sound intensity over time, and the Bioacoustic Index (BI), which summarises sound across frequencies. [SRC-019](sources.md#src-019), [SRC-034](sources.md#src-034), [SRC-045](sources.md#src-045)

**Mount Bold, South Australia.** Researchers made 240 recordings over five days in six plots. Two plots were cleared, two had been replanted and two retained woodland. Both indices rose with the number of animals counted. Only ACI showed a statistically significant association with richness. The field team distinguished several types of ant and spider, while counting some other animals in broader groups. Richness here measures the types distinguished in that survey. [SRC-019](sources.md#src-019), [SRC-034](sources.md#src-034), [SRC-055](sources.md#src-055)

**Greno Woods, South Yorkshire.** Here too, both indices rose with abundance. Neither showed a statistically significant link with richness. The team compared three restored plots with three recently felled plots. ACI and BI distinguished the two sets of plots when the team moved soil samples into a chamber that reduced outside noise. Neither index detected the same difference when the team recorded directly in the ground. Coauthor Carlos Abrahams was affiliated with Baker Consultants. [SRC-045](sources.md#src-045)

**Oxfordshire pasture.** A 2026 research briefing reported 24 hours of recording at each of ten grassland sites. Several indices correlated with earthworm counts from the same sites. The recording covered a full day of activity. The authors could not isolate the influence of farm management from other factors, including soil type and moisture. [SRC-021](sources.md#src-021)

### Can a recording identify what made a sound?

Researchers tested 14 indices on one-minute recordings they assembled from known sounds. Combinations of indices predicted the number of sound events more successfully than the variety of sounds across recordings or how evenly the sounds occurred. One animal can produce several events. A coauthor was affiliated with Baker Consultants. [SRC-020](sources.md#src-020)

Annells and colleagues tested whether movement alone could distinguish animals. They recorded ten individuals from each of six species, one at a time on an aluminium plate, for five minutes each. Across all six species, the researchers found differences in 19 measured sound features considered together. The comparison between garden worms and snails was inconclusive (*p* = 0.128). Identifying an animal in a recording from mixed soil would require a field test. [SRC-047](sources.md#src-047)

## Validation of the commercial score

The Soil Acoustic Meter (SAM) records vibrations. The supplier’s portal summarises each recording with its own Soil Acoustic Quality Index (SAQI). The developer reports controlled experiments and more than 2,000 recordings. The woodland and pasture studies used other indices. The public evidence reviewed gives no independent estimate of how accurately SAQI predicts animal counts or richness on a new farm. It also leaves open how much the score can change between repeat measurements at the same place. [SRC-002](sources.md#src-002), [SRC-003](sources.md#src-003), [SRC-009](sources.md#src-009), [SRC-020](sources.md#src-020), [SRC-023](sources.md#src-023), [SRC-045](sources.md#src-045)

The published Denton Reserve survey and international farm pilot describe differences in recordings. A proposed forest study names the commercial meter and sets out how researchers plan to compare sound with soil animals. The company page explains what each has reported. [Evidence from the supplier and its research partners](company-and-product.md#what-has-been-tested) · [SRC-051](sources.md#src-051), [SRC-054](sources.md#src-054)

## How to investigate a change in score

1. **How much do repeat readings vary?** Repeat the supplier’s sampling pattern at marked points under comparable conditions. Keep the individual readings as well as the average. At some points, reinsert the probe or repeat the measurement with another operator. The spread of readings helps establish whether a difference between visits deserves investigation. [SRC-003](sources.md#src-003), [SRC-004](sources.md#src-004), [SRC-022](sources.md#src-022)
2. **Does the score track the animals?** Pair each recording with an animal count at the same location. To check richness, also record which groups were present and how precisely they were identified. To establish performance on new farms, test on whole farms excluded from development. Report errors and uncertainty for each outcome, including quiet points. [Pairing a recording with an earthworm survey](monitoring-systems.md#what-to-compare-a-recording-against) · [SRC-019](sources.md#src-019), [SRC-020](sources.md#src-020), [SRC-045](sources.md#src-045), [SRC-046](sources.md#src-046)
3. **Did management improve the soil?** Follow a treated area and a comparable untreated area before and after a change. Measure the outcome itself, such as soil structure or availability of a particular nutrient. Differences in the recordings can help locate places to investigate. [SRC-021](sources.md#src-021), [SRC-023](sources.md#src-023), [SRC-024](sources.md#src-024)
